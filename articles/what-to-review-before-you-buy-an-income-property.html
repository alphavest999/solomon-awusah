What to Review Before You Buy an Income Property

By Dr. Solomon Chukwuemeka Awusah (Non-Clinical Strategist) · November 11, 2026

Buying an income property is often described as a numbers decision, but the numbers are only as good as the documents and observations behind them. A listing presents a property at its best. The work of due diligence is to replace that picture with an accurate one before you are committed. These are the areas worth reviewing carefully before you make or finalize an offer.

Verify the Income, Not Just the Rent Roll

A rent roll shows what tenants are supposed to pay. It does not show what they actually pay. Ask for bank deposit records or a ledger that shows collected rent over a meaningful period, and compare it line by line with the rent roll. Look for units that are vacant but listed as occupied, tenants who are consistently late, concessions that were given to fill units quickly, and rents that sit well above what similar nearby properties charge. Income that cannot be confirmed should be treated as income you may not receive.

Read Every Lease

Leases carry obligations that pass to the new owner. Review each one for the end date, renewal options, rent increase terms, security deposit amounts, and any promises the current owner made, such as included utilities, parking, or planned improvements. Watch for leases that are about to expire at the same time, because a cluster of move-outs can turn a stable property into a half-empty one within a few months. If a tenant has an unusual clause, it is better to learn about it now than after closing.

Rebuild the Operating Expenses Yourself

Seller expense figures are frequently incomplete. Property taxes may be based on an old assessment that will change after a sale. Insurance may be quoted at a rate you cannot match. Repairs may have been deferred, which makes recent expense totals look lower than normal. Build your own expense estimate from current quotes and local norms: taxes, insurance, utilities the owner pays, routine maintenance, turnover costs between tenants, management, and a reserve for larger items. Compare your version with the seller's and understand every difference.

Inspect the Building With Its Future in Mind

A general inspection is the starting point, not the finish line. For income property, the most expensive surprises tend to come from the roof, the foundation, plumbing and sewer lines, electrical systems, and heating and cooling equipment. Ask about the age of each major system and when it was last replaced or serviced. Walk through as many units as you can rather than relying on one or two that were prepared for showing. The goal is not to find a perfect building. It is to understand which large costs are likely in the next several years so they can be planned and priced into your decision.

Check the Paperwork Behind the Property

Title, survey, zoning, and permit records confirm that what you are buying matches what is being described. Make sure additions or converted spaces were permitted, that the current use is allowed, and that there are no open violations, liens, or easements that limit how the property can be used. Review service contracts for things like landscaping, waste removal, or laundry equipment, since some may transfer with the property. A qualified attorney and title professional should review these items; your role is to make sure nothing is skipped.

Understand the Neighborhood and the Market

A property's performance depends heavily on its surroundings. Visit at different times of day and on different days of the week. Talk with local property managers about typical vacancy, tenant demand, and what renters in the area expect. Look at what is being built nearby and whether new supply could compete for the same tenants. Consider how easy it is for residents to reach work, schools, shopping, and transportation. These factors shape both future rent and how quickly vacant units can be filled.

Conclusion

Careful review takes time, and it can feel like it is slowing down a good opportunity. In practice, it is what separates a sound purchase from an expensive lesson. Confirm the income, read the leases, rebuild the expenses, inspect thoroughly, check the records, and study the market. The property you buy will be the one the documents describe, so make sure you have read them.
