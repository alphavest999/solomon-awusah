How to Write Standard Operating Procedures Your Team Will Actually Use

By Dr. Solomon Chukwuemeka Awusah (Non-Clinical Strategist) · November 2, 2026

Most businesses have procedures. Fewer have procedures anyone reads. A binder on a shelf or a folder of documents nobody can find does little to keep work consistent. A good standard operating procedure, or SOP, is different: it is short, easy to locate, and written for the person doing the task on a busy day. Here is how to write SOPs that become part of how work actually gets done.

Start with the tasks that cause the most trouble

Trying to document everything at once usually produces a pile of half-finished documents. Begin instead with the tasks where mistakes are expensive, where results vary depending on who does the work, or where only one person knows the steps. Receiving inventory, closing out the month, onboarding a new tenant, and handing off a project are common examples. A handful of well-written procedures for high-impact tasks will do more good than dozens of rough ones.

Write with the people who do the work

The person who performs a task every week knows the shortcuts, the common problems and the steps that are easy to forget. Sit with them, watch the task being done, and write down what actually happens rather than what a manager assumes happens. Then ask them to review the draft. Procedures written this way are more accurate, and the team is far more likely to follow a document it helped create.

Keep each procedure short and specific

An SOP should cover one task from a clear starting point to a clear finish. If a document runs for many pages, it probably contains several procedures that should be split apart. A simple structure works well for most tasks:

State the purpose in one or two sentences, so the reader knows why the task matters. List what is needed before starting, such as access, tools, forms or information. Write the steps as numbered actions that begin with a verb, like “confirm,” “enter” or “send.” Describe what a finished result looks like, so the person knows when they are done. Note who to contact when something does not match the procedure.

Plain language matters. Avoid internal jargon where a common word will do, and define any term a new employee might not know. Screenshots, photos or a simple checklist often explain a step more clearly than another paragraph.

Make procedures easy to find

A procedure that takes ten minutes to locate will be skipped. Store SOPs in one shared place that everyone can reach, use consistent names, and organize them the way people think about their work, by department or by process. For tasks done away from a desk, such as in a warehouse, on a job site or at a property, consider printed checklists at the point of use or a version that opens easily on a phone.

Assign an owner and a review date

Procedures go out of date quietly. Software changes, a vendor is replaced, or the team finds a better way, and the document no longer matches reality. Once that happens, people stop trusting it. Give every SOP a named owner who is responsible for keeping it current, and record the date it was last reviewed. Set a regular review schedule, and update the document whenever the process changes rather than waiting for the next review.

Use procedures in training and in daily work

An SOP earns its value when it is used. Make procedures part of onboarding, so new team members learn the documented way from the start. When a mistake happens, check the procedure first: was it followed, was it unclear, or was it missing a step? Treat the answer as a chance to improve the document rather than to assign blame. Over time, this habit turns procedures into a living record of how the business works best.

Conclusion

Good standard operating procedures are not about paperwork. They protect quality when people are busy, make it easier to train new staff, and keep important knowledge from walking out the door when someone leaves. Start with the tasks that matter most, write them with the people who do the work, keep them short and easy to find, and give each one an owner. Procedures built this way become tools your team relies on rather than documents it ignores.
