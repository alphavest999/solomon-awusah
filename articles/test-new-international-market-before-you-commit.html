How to Test a New International Market Before You Commit to It

By Dr. Solomon Chukwuemeka Awusah (Non-Clinical Strategist) · November 9, 2026

Expanding into another country can look simple from a distance. There are customers, there is demand, and the product already works at home. The businesses that struggle abroad usually made that assumption and committed too much too early. A better approach is to treat a new market as a question to be answered with small, inexpensive tests before it becomes a large commitment.

Start with a specific reason to go

"We should be international" is not a strategy. Before researching any country, write down why expansion makes sense now and what it should accomplish. The reason might be demand you are already seeing from abroad, a domestic market that is getting crowded, a customer who wants you to follow them overseas, or a supply relationship that makes a certain region practical.

A specific reason also helps you choose. Instead of asking which country looks attractive in general, you can ask which one best serves the goal you wrote down. If nobody on your team can state that goal in a sentence or two, the idea is not ready to test.

Learn how customers actually buy there

Market size tells you very little about whether people will buy from you. What matters is how the purchase happens. Find out who makes the decision, what they use today, how they pay, and what would make them trust a company they have never heard of. Payment terms, buying channels and expectations about service often differ more than the product requirements do.

The most useful research is direct conversation. Speak with potential customers, distributors and other businesses already operating there, and ask what surprised them. Listen for the reasons someone would say no. A polite expression of interest is not the same as a purchase, and in some business cultures it may simply be good manners.

Map the full cost of getting there

The price of a product abroad is rarely the home price plus shipping. Build a landed-cost picture that includes freight, insurance, duties, local taxes, warehousing, distributor margins, currency conversion and the cost of handling returns. Then add the less visible costs: translation, local customer support, travel and the management time the effort will absorb.

Run the numbers at the price the local market will accept, not the price you would like to charge. If the margin only works when everything goes right, you have learned something valuable before spending much at all.

Find out which rules apply before you need them

Every market has its own requirements for product standards, labeling, data handling, contracts, employment and tax registration. You do not need to master these yourself, but you do need to know which ones apply to your specific plan, and which would change if you moved from selling remotely to placing people or inventory in the country. Consult qualified local advisers early. Their answers may change the entry route you choose, and those conversations are far cheaper before a contract is signed than after.

Run a small, time-limited test

Choose the lightest version of entry that will produce real evidence. Depending on the business, that could be a limited shipment through an established distributor, a pilot with a handful of customers, a short online sales campaign aimed at one country, or a service engagement delivered remotely. Avoid long exclusive agreements at this stage, because they are hard to unwind and can block better partners later.

Keep the test narrow: one market, one offer, one defined period and one person accountable for the outcome. Spreading a small effort across several countries usually produces thin results that are impossible to interpret.

Decide in advance what success looks like

Before the test starts, write down what you will measure and what result would justify the next step. Useful measures include repeat orders, actual margin after all costs, time spent resolving problems and how much attention the effort took from the core business. Set a date for the decision and name who will make it.

This matters because enthusiasm grows once people have invested time in a project. A written standard makes it easier to stop, adjust or continue based on what happened rather than on what the team hoped would happen. Stopping a test is not a failure. It is the test doing its job.

International growth rewards patience more than speed. A company that answers its questions cheaply, one market at a time, keeps the option to go further and keeps its home business strong while it learns. Commit seriously only after the evidence has earned it.
