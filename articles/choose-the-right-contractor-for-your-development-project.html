How to Choose the Right Contractor for Your Development Project

By Dr. Solomon Chukwuemeka Awusah (Non-Clinical Strategist) · November 25, 2026

Choosing a general contractor is one of the most consequential decisions in any development project. The contractor turns drawings and budgets into a physical building, and the quality of that relationship shapes cost, schedule and the finished result. Price matters, but the lowest bid is rarely the full story. A careful selection process protects the owner long before construction begins.

Define the Project Before You Ask for Bids

Contractors can only price what they can see. If drawings are incomplete, scope is vague or finishes are undecided, every bid will include guesses, and those guesses will differ from one contractor to the next. That makes bids hard to compare and invites change orders later.

Before going out to bid, assemble a clear package: current drawings, a written scope of work, a finish schedule, the expected start date and any site constraints such as access, staging space or working hours. The more complete the package, the more meaningful the numbers that come back.

Look Beyond the Bottom Line

When bids arrive, resist the urge to sort them by total price and stop there. Read each one line by line. Look for exclusions, allowances and assumptions. One contractor may include site cleanup, permits or temporary utilities while another leaves them out. An unusually low bid often means something was missed or will be billed separately later.

Ask each bidder to walk you through their numbers. A contractor who can explain how they arrived at a figure, and where the risks sit, is usually one who has thought carefully about your project.

Check Experience That Matches Your Project

A contractor who builds excellent single-family homes may not be the right fit for a mixed-use building, and a firm used to large commercial work may not give a small project the attention it needs. Ask for examples of completed projects similar in size, type and complexity to yours.

Then verify what you hear. Visit finished projects when you can. Speak with past owners and ask direct questions: Did the project finish on time? How were surprises handled? Would they hire this contractor again? Confirm licensing, insurance and bonding with the issuing sources rather than relying on copies alone.

Evaluate the Team, Not Just the Company

The firm's reputation is important, but the people assigned to your job will determine your daily experience. Ask who the project manager and site superintendent will be, how many other projects they will be handling at the same time and how often they will be on site.

Meet these people before you sign. Pay attention to how they communicate, how they respond to questions and whether they raise concerns on their own. A superintendent who flags problems early is worth far more than one who simply says everything is fine.

Put Expectations in Writing

A good contract does more than state a price. It defines scope, schedule, payment terms, the process for approving change orders, how delays are handled and what documentation the contractor will provide along the way. It should also be clear about warranty terms and what closeout looks like, including final inspections, as-built drawings and operating manuals for building systems.

Agree on a reporting rhythm from the start. Regular progress meetings, written updates and photos help everyone stay aligned and make it easier to address issues while they are still small. Owners should have contracts reviewed by qualified legal counsel before signing.

Plan for the Relationship, Not Just the Transaction

Even well-planned projects encounter surprises. What matters is how the owner and contractor work through them together. Contractors are more likely to be candid about problems when they trust that the owner will respond fairly and make decisions promptly.

Owners can support that trust by paying approved invoices on schedule, answering questions quickly and keeping one clear point of contact. Slow decisions on the owner's side can cause delays that are later blamed on the contractor, and those disputes help no one.

Conclusion

Selecting a contractor is not a single decision made on bid day. It is a process that begins with a clear project definition and continues through careful review of bids, verification of experience, evaluation of the people involved and a contract that sets expectations plainly. Owners who invest time in that process give their projects a far better chance of finishing on budget, on schedule and built to the standard they intended.
